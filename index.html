The asset

A rent roll used to be a private ledger. Today it is a product — and the businesses that sell the product are worth billions. CoStar Group did not invent rent data. It standardized it, verified it, and turned it into a subscription. The category it created now needs its name.

Commercial rent data is infrastructure. Lenders price CMBS off it. REITs mark portfolios to it. Brokers justify valuations with it. Every participant rents the truth — at prices that renew, annually, in the high five to six figures per seat.

CoStar's data business posted nearly $2.7 billion in 2024 revenue and carries a valuation in the tens of billions — built entirely out of other people's buildings' numbers. Moody's paid about $278 million for REIS in 2018, the oldest rent database in the country. Category-defining data businesses don't just survive downturns; vacancies and dispersion make them more necessary.

"When the spread between trophy rents and average rents is 100 percent, every participant needs to know precisely where the line is."

Exact-match .com names are the scarcest digital real estate in a category. Voice.com sold for $30 million in 2019. Cars.com was valued at $872 million in the 2017 Gannett deal. The buyers who understand CRE data already know what a name like this is worth to them.

The data moat

Why rent data compounds — and why the name that describes it appreciates with it.

i.

Renewal economics. Rent intelligence is bought on annual subscription and renewed at rates in the high 80s percent. Customers don't churn from infrastructure they mark their books with.

ii.

The dataset gets richer as it's dug. Every new lease record makes the historical series more valuable — forecasting requires history. Marginal cost falls while marginal value rises.

iii.

Volatility is demand. Record vacancy, 80% Class B value write-downs, and a 100% trophy-to-average rent spread are not headwinds for data. They are the raw material it sells.

iv.

Names are compounding too. Premium exact-match .com domains have traded up for three decades. The longer the category exists, the fewer category names remain.
