The Asking Rent Is a Fiction

CBRE's Q3 2026 numbers look like a recovery. Strip out the concessions and the rent tenants actually pay tells a different story — and that gap is the business.

Analysis · November 2026 · 4 min read

The broker's email arrived on a Tuesday in October with a number designed to stop a negotiation: $52 a square foot, Midtown, full floor, fifteen years. The tenant's advisor ran the same deal through a different spreadsheet and got $44. Both spreadsheets used the broker's own figures. The difference was everything the brochure left out: fourteen months of free rent, $95 a foot in tenant improvements, a landlord contribution to the tenant's moving costs, and a termination option priced so generously it functioned as a second lease. The asking rent was $52. The rent, measured the way a lender or a buyer would measure it, was something else entirely.

CBRE's Q3 2026 U.S. office report gives the national version of that story. Overall vacancy fell to 18.2%, its fifth straight quarterly decline, and prime vacancy dropped 250 basis points in a year to 11.9%. Midtown Manhattan prime vacancy stood at just 1.9%. Average asking rent rose 3.1% year over year to $37.95 a square foot — the fastest increase since 2016 — and leasing hit 63.8 million square feet in the quarter, the highest since late 2024. CBRE expects full-year leasing to beat the 2022 record, and forecasts office investment volume rising 10% in 2026 to $91.1 billion. Read as a headline, that is a recovery.

Read as a rent roll, it is thinner. CBRE's own spread between asking rents and the rents tenants actually pay narrowed only slightly, to 9.9% — still above the 8.6% spread of 2019, after six years in which landlords supposedly regained pricing power. JLL's read on trophy space is sharper still: effective rents for first-generation trophy space rose more than 20% year over year, while second-generation trophy rents rose about 10%, because tenants priced out of the best buildings bid up the next tier. The market is not one market. It is a trophy market with scarcity pricing, and everything else, where concessions do the negotiating that the asking rent will not admit to.

Concessions are the real rent

This is why a database that records asking rents is a database of advertisements. Tenant improvement allowances are capital the landlord spends to win the tenant; free rent is income the landlord forgoes; both come straight out of the deal's economics, and neither appears in the asking figure. A landlord offering $52 with fourteen months free and $95 in improvements is, over a ten-year term, charging materially less than a landlord offering $48 straight — and any underwriting model that treats those two deals as $52 versus $48 will misprice the building, the loan, and the tenant's true occupancy cost. The brokers know this. The lenders know this. The published data mostly does not.

The counterargument deserves a hearing, because landlords make it with some justice: improvements are not rent, they are investment in the asset, and a tenant that receives a new fit-out has received value that a straight-rent comparison ignores. A landlord who spends $95 a foot is left with a better building at term end; the tenant who takes $48 and pays its own fit-out is left with nothing. Fair enough — but that defense concedes the point. If improvements are asset value, then the asking rent alone cannot tell a buyer what the building earns, and a data product that reports it without the concession package is reporting half a deal and calling it the whole.

What the next rent database has to do

The fix is not more asking rents, collected faster. It is effective rent, lease by lease: face rent, free-rent months, improvement dollars, escalations, options, and the term over which each is amortized — the same fields a CMBS analyst reconstructs from loan documents and a REIT analyst reconstructs from supplemental filings, done once and shared. The firms that build that record first will set the reference price for the next cycle of office underwriting, because every participant in the deal already does this math privately and hates doing it. The asking rent will keep its place on the brochure. The business is in the number underneath it.
