The Conversion Question: What Happens to the Other 80% of the Office Market

Manhattan trophy vacancy is 4.9% and deals are clearing above $300 a square foot. The rest of the office market — the Class B and C buildings nobody is converting fast enough — is where the rent data gets honest.

Analysis · November 2026 · 4 min read

The law firm Simpson Thacher & Bartlett signed for 916,000 square feet across 26 floors at Extell Development’s 570 Fifth Avenue. Bank of America added 600,000 square feet to its existing 1.8-million-square-foot position at One Bryant Park. In the same city, in the same quarter, landlords of Class B buildings that were asking roughly $75 a square foot a year ago began telling tenants to expect something near $125 — a level some of those tenants cannot sustain over the life of a lease. Manhattan’s office market in the third quarter of 2026 was not one market. It was two, and the distance between them is the story.

The trophy market is sold out

JLL puts Manhattan trophy vacancy at a cycle-low 4.9%, with trophy asking rents averaging $137.09 a square foot. Avison Young has trophy availability at just 10.1%. Manhattan overall vacancy dropped to 12.6% in the third quarter — the lowest reading since before 2020, down from 13.1% at the close of the second quarter and 14.7% a year earlier — while direct asking rent climbed to $84.56 a square foot, and transactions above $300 a square foot are now common enough that the conversation has shifted to whether $400 is achievable. Cushman & Wakefield puts Midtown’s average asking rent at $78.83 a square foot, with Class A at $90.57, its highest since 2008. CBRE, which tracks only fully modernized buildings, had Midtown at $85.53 with 12.1% availability. The return to five-day office schedules is, by now, less a policy debate than an operating assumption: companies competing for elite workers across finance, insurance, law and technology have moved to full-time requirements and discovered that top performers expect dedicated workstations rather than shared desks.

Nationally, the same split is visible in softer focus. CBRE’s third-quarter 2026 figures show asking rents up 3.1% year over year to $37.95 a square foot, the fastest pace since 2016 and above the 30-year average of 2%, with vacancy at 18.2% and net absorption positive for a tenth straight quarter — 4.9 million square feet in the quarter, 29.2 million over four quarters. JLL counted 6.0 million square feet of net absorption in the quarter and 21 million year to date, with leasing volume up 6.4% over twelve months, gateway markets up 13%, and Sun Belt and smaller tertiary markets back above 95% of their 2019 leasing volume. The recovery is real. It is also selective, and the selection is the point.

The pipeline won’t save it

Manhattan has approximately 7.9 million square feet under construction, with delivery expected between 2028 and 2032 — but a significant share of that inventory is already pre-leased. JLL counts a 9.7 million square foot pipeline that is 60.9% preleased. The trophy space that is coming is, in effect, already spoken for, which compresses the pipeline of available trophy product before it arrives. CoStar’s Victor Rodriguez put the risk plainly: as pre-leased trophy space becomes scarcer, the market will generate fewer headline-making full-building transactions, and the scarcity premium will rise further. The buildings that would relieve the pressure are the ones that will not be ready for three to six years, and the tenants signing $300-a-foot deals are not waiting.

The stranded middle

Chicago shows what happens to the rest. The central business district’s direct vacancy rate was flat in the third quarter at 26.6%, but trophy vacancy fell to 11.4%, the lowest since the first quarter of 2023 — against 23.6% for non-trophy Class A buildings. The overall average asking rent was $45.60 a square foot, up 0.7% on the quarter and 0.5% on the year, but trophy buildings averaged $73.51, a $22.79 premium over non-trophy Class A at $50.72. Fulton Market trophy asking rents reached $81.25 a square foot and River North $79.63. The spread between the best and the rest is now wider than the rent on the rest. In Ottawa, the same pattern in miniature: Class A vacancy 13.1%, Class B 15.0%, Class C 25.6%, with net asking rents essentially flat year over year at $17.40 a square foot despite a federal return-to-office mandate.

The standard answer for the stranded middle is conversion — turn the obsolete office into apartments. The standard answer is also slow, expensive, and selective: floor plates built for trading floors do not divide into bedrooms, plumbing stacks do not move cheaply, and the buildings that convert best are the ones in residential neighborhoods with the right bones, not the ones in office districts with the worst vacancy. The Prime Group, an investment partner in the Thompson Center redevelopment, acquired 55 W. Monroe in Chicago and plans to modernize its amenities to align with tenant expectations — a repositioning, not a conversion, and a sign that even sophisticated capital prefers to fix the office rather than erase it. JPMorgan’s recommitment to its Central Loop space at 10 S Dearborn points the same way.

The case that the middle recovers

The counterargument deserves its weight. Leasing is broadening: JLL characterized third-quarter Manhattan demand as broad-based across major industries, not a law-firm story alone, and leasing volume rose 2.3% year over year even as vacancy fell. CBRE forecasts annual office investment volume rising 10% in 2026 to $91.1 billion, which means capital is returning to the sector, not just to its trophies. Gateway markets are up 13% on leasing, and Sun Belt and tertiary markets are back above 95% of 2019 volume — the recovery is spreading outward from the coasts. A Class B landlord who modernizes — real amenities, real air, real light — can still lease, and the buildings that do are the ones whose vacancy falls first.

But modernization is a capital decision, and capital follows the spread. A landlord choosing between a $95-a-foot tenant improvement package on a $52 asking rent and a conversion study that takes two years is choosing between two kinds of risk, and the market is telling them which one pays. The honest read of the third quarter is that the office market is now two markets with one average: a trophy market that is effectively sold out, and a commodity market whose rents are still a fiction until the concessions are counted. The average — $37.95 nationally, $84.56 in Manhattan — describes neither of them. Price the building you actually own, in the market it actually sits in, and the data gets honest in a hurry.
