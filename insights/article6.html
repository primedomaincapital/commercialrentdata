What Do Industrial Rents Cost Per Square Foot in 2026?

The national average is $10.40 to $11.16 a square foot — and it describes almost no building in the country. The real rent is set by the metro, the bay size, the clear height, and whether the building has the power.

Analysis · November 2026 · 10 min read

In the third quarter of 2023, a tenant signing for warehouse space in Los Angeles County agreed to $1.71 a square foot a month. Three years later, the county's average direct asking rent is $1.19 a month — down 30.4%, according to CBRE's third-quarter 2026 Los Angeles industrial figures. The tenant who signed at the peak is now renegotiating into a market that has given back nearly a third of its rent, while a tenant signing the same week in Miami-Dade is paying $16.84 a square foot a year, and that number is still climbing. Industrial rent in 2026 is not a number. It is a map, a building specification, and a negotiation — and the national average describes almost none of it.

Three national numbers, one market

Start with the headline, and then distrust it. CBRE's third-quarter 2026 industrial report puts the national average net asking rent at $11.16 a square foot, up 3.6% year over year, with vacancy at 6.4% — down 20 basis points on the year — and availability at 8.9%. JLL's reading of the same quarter is $10.55 a square foot, up 3.2% year over year, with vacancy at 6.8%, down 80 basis points. Cushman & Wakefield has vacancy at 6.8% as well, but asking rents at $10.40 a square foot triple-net, up 4% on the year. Three firms, three averages, a spread of 76 cents — and all three pointing the same way.

The direction is the story. Rent growth had slowed to roughly 1% in the second half of 2025; Cushman & Wakefield now has it at 4%, and JLL calls its 3.2% the fastest pace since the second quarter of 2024. Demand did the work. CBRE counted 104.6 million square feet of net absorption in the quarter — the highest quarterly total since the fourth quarter of 2022, and well above the ten-year quarterly average of 82.9 million — Taking year-to-date absorption to 237.8 million square feet, against 97.7 million a year earlier. JLL recorded 202.6 million square feet of leasing in the quarter, up 38.5% year over year, with year-to-date net absorption of 231.6 million square feet already above the whole of 2025. Colliers, tracking big-box space, found 88 million square feet absorbed in the first half of 2026 against 45 million square feet of new supply. Moody's Analytics has projected industrial rent growth of roughly 3% for 2026, the highest of any commercial real estate category — a forecast reported in trade coverage, not a headline figure from Moody's, and worth treating as a directional call rather than a measurement.

Why the averages disagree is itself useful information. CBRE's figure is a net asking rent across its tracked inventory; JLL's is an average asking rent on a different inventory cut; Cushman & Wakefield's is triple-net. Big-box space, small-bay space, and flex space are different products with different rents, and each firm's mix weights the average differently. The honest way to quote the national industrial rent in 2026 is as a range — $10.40 to $11.16 a square foot — with growth of 3% to 4% and vacancy of 6.4% to 6.8%. One number to two decimal places is precision the market does not have.

The map: $5.59 in Kansas City, $16.84 in Miami

The range across metros is wider than the range across research firms, and it is where the money is made or lost. CBRE's third-quarter 2026 figures put Kansas City at $5.59 a square foot, up 3.0% on the quarter. Chicago, per JLL, averages $8.46 with vacancy at 4.4% — down for a fifth consecutive quarter — after 16.3 million square feet of leasing in the quarter took year-to-date volume to 41.3 million, past the totals for 2024 and 2025 combined. Sacramento sits at $0.82 a square foot a month triple-net, or $9.84 a year, per CBRE. Orlando averages $10.48, per CBRE, with vacancy at 10.6%. Dallas–Fort Worth runs about $10.36 to $10.39 triple-net, per CBRE and Matthews, with vacancy near 7.8% against 8.9% a year earlier. Los Angeles County, at $1.19 a month, works out to $14.28 a year. Miami-Dade averages $16.84, per CBRE's first-quarter 2026 figures — Cushman & Wakefield's warehouse and distribution measure for the same market is $15.61 — with vacancy near 6.8%.

Growth is even more uneven than the level. Cushman & Wakefield counts 13 of 83 markets posting double-digit rent growth, led by Dallas–Fort Worth at 20%. At the same time the firm notes rents softening in some coastal markets in California, New Jersey, and Miami — the places where the post-pandemic surge was steepest and the new supply landed hardest. The pattern rhymes with the office market, where CBRE's third-quarter 2026 figures show asking rents at $37.95 a square foot even as vacancy sits at 18.2%.

Building type moves the number as much as the metro does. In Orlando, Lee & Associates put flex asking rates at $16.11 a square foot in the second quarter of 2026 — against roughly $10 to $10.50 for the industrial market overall — with flex vacancy at 4.84% while the metro ran 10.6%. Small-bay and flex space is the tightest slice of the sector: tenants who need 5,000 square feet near the customer pay a 50% premium over the big-box rate, and they pay it because the alternative is a 100,000-square-foot box twenty miles further out. A tenant who quotes the metro average to a small-bay landlord is negotiating against the wrong number.

The building sets the rent now

The plan brief for this article said power availability and clear heights now move the number more than location alone. The evidence supports it — with a caveat about how much of that evidence is measured and how much is asserted. The physical facts are not in dispute. The modern logistics standard is 32- to 40-foot clear heights; legacy stock built before 2010 runs 24 to 28 feet. A 36-foot-clear building stacks roughly 30% more pallets than a 28-foot building on the same footprint, according to a Florida port-market analysis of the new generation of product — an engineering estimate, not a broker survey, but the arithmetic is straightforward and nobody in the business disputes the direction. Two Prologis listings show what the standard looks like in practice: the Flagler Station project in Medley, Florida, offers two planned buildings with 36-foot clear heights and up to 4,000 amps of power at 480 volts three-phase; Prologis's South Brunswick, New Jersey, building pairs a 36-foot clear height with 2,600 amps.

Power is the sharper edge. Automation-ready buildings are specified at 4,000 to 8,000 or more amps, and automated storage and retrieval systems can require many times the power of a conventional warehouse — trade coverage of Prologis research puts automated facilities at roughly 10% higher rents than non-automated peers, and power availability in the first rank of site-selection criteria alongside transport and labour, according to Link Logistics. Treat both figures as second-hand: they reach this article through trade summaries of Prologis and Link Logistics material, not through published Prologis or Link Logistics reports, and the 10% premium is a research finding about automated facilities, not a market-wide rent index. What is first-hand is the spillover JLL reports: a shortage of modern industrial facilities is pushing up asking rents in some areas, in part because data-center development is creating demand from equipment suppliers, construction vendors, and power-infrastructure companies. Prologis — the world's largest industrial REIT — filed permits in March 2026 to develop a data-center and industrial complex in Mississauga, Ontario, and trade coverage puts hyperscaler infrastructure spending for 2026 above $690 billion. A warehouse with secured power is no longer just a warehouse. It is an option on the tenant who needs the power most.

The tightest slice of the market tells the same story in vacancy terms. JLL puts vacancy in Class A properties larger than one million square feet at 5.4%, down from 9.5% a year earlier. The newest, tallest, best-powered buildings are leasing while the older stock waits — the industrial version of the office market's flight to quality, on a narrower spread.

Asking is not effective

Every rent in this article so far is an asking rent, and asking rents in industrial are quoted triple-net — the tenant pays taxes, insurance, and common-area maintenance on top of the base rate. A Dallas–Fort Worth tenant looking at two buildings quoted at the same $10.25 base rate can end up $0.60 apart once operating-expense estimates are added, according to a practitioner walkthrough of DFW proposals — an illustration from one brokerage's deals, not a market statistic, but a fair warning about what the base rate leaves out. Add the expense load, amortize the tenant-improvement allowance over the term, and subtract whatever free rent was conceded, and the $10.40-to-$11.16 national average becomes something like $12 or more all-in for many tenants — and less, in markets where landlords are still conceding, for others.

Concessions are thinner here than in offices, and that matters for anyone comparing the two. The office market's asking-taking spread sits near 10%, as this site's earlier article on asking rents documented — the brochure rent is the least reliable number in that sector. Industrial has no equivalent cushion to strip away: JLL reports concessions stable in Chicago, and small tenants in Orlando are told to expect less free rent than big-box tenants get. The industrial asking rent is closer to the effective rent than the office asking rent is — which means there is less to negotiate away from the number on the page.

The case that rents have already peaked

The counterargument deserves its weight, and Los Angeles is its best exhibit. A 30.4% fall from the third-quarter 2023 peak is not a softening; it is a repricing, and it happened in the country's largest industrial market. The construction pipeline JLL counts at 317 million square feet is still delivering, and vacancy of 6.4% to 6.8% — while low by office standards — is well above the sub-4% readings of the 2021–22 peak. Cushman & Wakefield's note that rents softened in some coastal California, New Jersey, and Miami markets is the other half of the Dallas–Fort Worth 20% story: the same supply wave that lifted the national average in the Sun Belt is pressing on the coasts.

The honest read of the third quarter is that rent growth re-accelerated — from roughly 1% to roughly 4% — but it re-accelerated narrowly. Modern, tall, well-powered product in growing metros is tight and getting more expensive. Older, shorter, under-powered product in supplied markets is competing on price. A landlord who owns the second group and underwrites the first group's rent is making the same mistake as the office landlord who underwrote the trophy rent for a Class B building.

For a tenant, the practical conclusion is to underwrite cubic feet, amps, and operating expenses — not the headline rate and not the zip code. A 36-foot-clear building at $11 a foot can be cheaper per pallet than a 28-foot building at $9, and a building with 4,000 amps already installed is worth more than the same building with a two-year utility interconnection queue. For a landlord, the conclusion is blunter: the specification is the rent now. A 1990s building with 24-foot clears and 1,200 amps competes on price alone. And for anyone buying or selling rent data, the spread is the product — $5.59 in Kansas City, $16.84 in Miami, a 50% premium for small-bay over big-box in the same metro. A dataset that can show a tenant why their building costs what it costs is worth more than one that can only show them the average.
