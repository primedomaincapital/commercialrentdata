How Long Is a Commercial Lease Really? Term Trends by Property Type

One tenant just signed for 25 years in Manhattan. Two-thirds of new British retail leases run under three. The average lease term no longer describes either of them — and the gap is repricing landlord risk.

Analysis · November 2026 · 10 min read

In the second quarter of 2026, Simpson Thacher & Bartlett signed a 25-year lease for 916,000 square feet at 570 Fifth Avenue in Manhattan. In the same quarter, two-thirds of the new retail leases signed in Britain ran for less than three years. Both deals are real. Both are rational. And together they describe a commercial lease market that no longer has a standard term — it has a negotiation, and the negotiation now moves the value of the building.

The averages, by property type

The first honest thing to say is that no American data series tracks average lease term by property type the way CBRE tracks asking rents. The United States has broker transaction samples and a quarterly New York report; the cleanest quarterly term series anywhere comes from Re-Leased, a lease-management platform, and it covers Britain and Australia — not the United States. Here is what the sources actually support, with the geography attached to every figure.

In the United States, the ranges are wide and the samples are small. An investment-manager guide from BAM Capital puts typical lease duration at 5–15 years for office, 5–10 years for retail, and 3–10 years for industrial — a useful shape, not a measurement. Link Logistics puts standard warehouse terms at three to ten years, with five the most common. CBRE's analysis of 3,900 office lease transactions across 12 U.S. markets found an average office term of 9.2 years, with Manhattan's top 2025 deals averaging more than 17 years — numbers that reach this article second-hand, through a syndicated trade feature summarizing CBRE's analysis, and should be read as a transaction sample, not a national average. The one hard quarterly U.S. series is Tri State Commercial Realty's New York report: the average lease term in Manhattan was 7 years in the second quarter of 2026, unchanged from the first quarter and from a year earlier; Brooklyn averaged 7 years and Queens 5.

Re-Leased's Market Lens for the third quarter of 2026 — British data, drawn from leases on its platform — puts the averages at 53.2 months for industrial, 43.4 months for office, and 36.1 months for retail. The Australian edition puts retail at 34.4 months. The ordering is the same in every dataset: industrial longest, office in the middle, retail shortest. The levels differ — a platform's signed leases are not a broker's closed transactions — but the shape is consistent.

The short end is where the action is

Retail is moving fastest, and it is moving toward the tenant. For years the expectation in American retail leasing was a base term of ten years or longer — a decade of liability before a retailer had proved the location, as one law-firm client note put it in November 2026. The tenant-favorable structure now being negotiated is a three- to five-year initial term with multiple renewal options, putting control of the future in the tenant's hands. Shorter terms, kick-out clauses, and sales-based rents are becoming more common across the sector, according to trade coverage of the shift, with many retailers preferring lower base rents plus a percentage-of-sales component.

The numbers behind that description are stark. In Britain, the average new retail lease fell 11.4% year over year to 36.1 months in the third quarter of 2026 — the lowest third-quarter figure since the series began, and roughly half the 73.3 months recorded in the third quarter of 2019. Two-thirds of new retail leases completed in the quarter ran for less than three years, and the growth came from one- to three-year terms, not from sub-twelve-month deals. In Australia, almost half of new retail leases ran for under three years and the average fell 6.8% to 34.4 months; across suburban south-east Queensland, the five-year lease has gone from the default to the exception — 56.0% of small retail leases in 2022, 18.3% in 2026 to date, with three-year terms now leading at 25.3% and 65.3% of leases signed this year running three years or less.

Office is shortening more slowly, and from a longer base — but it is shortening. In Britain, the average office lease length rose 6.1% year over year to 43.4 months in the third quarter, yet 46.9% of office leases starting in the quarter still ran for under three years, and the average remains well below pre-pandemic levels. In Australia, one- to three-year terms were the most common band in all three sectors, and average lease lengths are still about a third lower than in the third quarter of 2019. The mechanism is visible in JLL's research on San Juan's office market: typical five-year terms carry three-year termination clauses and ten-year terms carry seven-year outs. The headline term says five years. The effective commitment says three.

Tom Wallace, chief executive of Re-Leased, put the consequence plainly in the firm's third-quarter release: shorter terms change the rhythm of portfolio management, bringing the next negotiation, the next renewal, and the next income decision closer. His warning was aimed at landlords who read high rent collection as safety — collection ran at 97.8% for office, 97.6% for retail, and 96.2% for industrial in the British data. The rent due today is being paid; the rent due in three years is an open question.

The long end hasn't gone away

None of this means the long lease is dead. It means the long lease has become a price, not a default. Simpson Thacher's 25-year commitment at 570 Fifth Avenue is the clearest American example: a law firm that knows it will be in Manhattan in 2051 pays for certainty with duration, and Extell Development, the building's developer, gets a quarter-century of income to finance against. Manhattan's top 2025 deals averaged more than 17 years in the CBRE sample cited above — the tenants landlords compete for still sign the longest terms.

Industrial is where the long term is most defensible, and the mechanics explain why. Standard warehouse leases run three to ten years, but build-to-suit deals — where the landlord constructs a facility to the tenant's specification — typically run ten to twenty years in Dallas–Fort Worth, with twelve to eighteen months from letter of intent to occupancy. Industrial terms of seven to fifteen years are normal in renewal practice, and the renewal itself is a once-a-decade decision: the tenant's racking and conveyors are engineered to one building, relocation budgets for a 200,000-square-foot operation commonly run to seven figures, and the option window typically opens twelve months before expiry and closes at nine or six. A tenant who has bolted their business to a building does not renegotiate every three years. They cannot afford to.

The market structure supports the long end, too. JLL puts vacancy in Class A properties larger than one million square feet at 5.4%, down from 9.5% a year earlier — the scarcest modern space still commands term. The logic runs in reverse in offices: with national vacancy at 18.2% and asking rents at $37.95 a square foot in CBRE's third-quarter 2026 figures, and San Francisco vacancy at 26.7% as of September 2026, the landlord of a half-empty building buys the tenant's signature with flexibility. Term follows leverage, and leverage follows vacancy.

What a break option costs the landlord

This is where the shorter term quietly reprices the building. A lease is not just rent; it is a schedule of future income, and every break option and every three-year renewal shortens the schedule the valuer can rely on. The costs arrive in three forms. First, re-letting: each expiry means a new tenant-improvement package and a new free-rent concession, and fit-out is not getting cheaper — Cushman & Wakefield's 2026 fit-out cost guide has U.S. costs up 5% year over year, with New York at $212.59 a square foot and San Francisco at $219.26, as reported in trade coverage of the guide. Second, downtime: breaking a lease typically costs six to twelve months of rent in buyout fees, sublease blocks average 18 months on the market, and sublet space clears at 20% to 40% discounts — figures from the same syndicated analysis, worth treating as market colour rather than audited benchmarks. Third, footprint: JLL found enterprise tenants larger than 25,000 square feet cut their footprints by 7.9% on average when leases expired in 2024. A landlord who signs a three-year term is not just accepting three years of rent. They are accepting a 7.9% smaller tenant at the next negotiation, if the tenant comes back at all.

Retention data from Re-Leased sharpens the point. Over the twelve months to September 2026 in Britain, industrial recorded the highest tenant retention at 81% and rent retention at 88.9%, followed by office at 78.1% and 85.2%, with retail lowest at 74.7% and 79.3%. The sector with the longest leases keeps the most tenants and the most rent — partly selection, since stable tenants sign long, and partly structure. The landlord's income is only as durable as the term behind it, and the term is getting shorter in exactly the sectors where retention is weakest.

Lenders have noticed. Trepp's office CMBS delinquency rate set a record at 12.34% in January 2026 and stood at 12.16% in September — and lease term is one of the first assumptions a valuer discounts. A ten-year lease to a credit tenant is underwriting. A three-year lease with a break at year two is a hope. The gap between those two things is now priced into the building, whether or not it appears in the asking rent.

The case for the long lease

The counterargument deserves its weight, because the long lease is not nostalgia — it is arithmetic. A $212.59-a-square-foot fit-out in New York does not amortize over a three-year term; it barely amortizes over five. Lenders underwrite term: a landlord financing a repositioning needs contracted income that outlives the loan, and a tenant who wants the landlord to fund the build-out pays with duration. The CBRE sample's 9.2-year average office term says the market still clears long where the tenant is strong and the space is specific. Manhattan's 7-year average term, unchanged across four quarters, says the center is holding.

The fair summary is that the market has split, as this site's earlier articles documented in rents. Anchor tenants and build-to-suit industrial users sign long, because they must. Small and mid-size tenants, retailers, and anyone whose headcount or sales are uncertain sign short, with options, because the last five years taught them what a ten-year commitment costs when the world changes in year two. The average term, whatever dataset you use, now describes neither group. It describes a market where duration has become a negotiated price, like rent itself — and like rent, it is moving in the tenant's favour everywhere except the scarcest buildings.

For a tenant, the conclusion is to buy flexibility with rent rather than with personal guarantees: a higher face rent on a three-year term with a renewal option is usually cheaper than a lower rent on a ten-year term with a buyout clause, once the cost of exit is counted. For a landlord, it is to underwrite rollover as a recurring cost — tenant improvements, free rent, downtime, a smaller footprint — rather than a one-off event, and to price the break option explicitly instead of giving it away to win the signature. For an investor, it is to read the lease expiry schedule before the rent roll: two buildings at the same rent are different assets if one re-prices next year and the other in 2035. And for anyone in the business of rent data, the missing dataset is now obvious. Rents are published quarterly by every major brokerage. Lease terms are not — they live in broker samples, platform data, and one New York report. The firm that publishes a proper lease-term series, by property type, by market, every quarter, will own the next argument in commercial real estate.
